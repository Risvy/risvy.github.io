Research

How older adults decide whether to trust the technologies designed for them.

Trust and distrust in technologies for aging

ACM ASSETS 2026, first author

Unpacked how distrust takes shape for older adults: when a system's workings become illegible, as trust builds and breaks over time, and as a way of setting boundaries around how far technology reaches. Turned those patterns into design directions.

Paper

Three patterns

  1. Legibility

    Distrust emerges when the workings of a system become illegible: when a person cannot tell where their information is stored, or trace where it moves.

  2. Time

    Trust is not settled once. It builds through prior experience and shifts with breakdowns, so the same person can rely on a system in one phase of use and withhold that reliance in the next.

  3. Boundaries

    Distrust can also be deliberate boundary work: a way of limiting how far a technology reaches into a person's autonomy, relationships, and everyday life.

These patterns point toward systems that support trust across the phases of use, and that make the movement and persistence of information perceptible rather than hidden.

Publications

Towards Designing for (Dis)Trust in Technologies for Aging

Muhid Hassan Risvy, Dimitri Theodoratos, Alisha Pradhan

Proceedings of the 28th International ACM SIGACCESS Conference on Computers and Accessibility (ASSETS 2026). Accepted.

arXiv preprint

Across cohorts of older adults and generations of technology, (dis)trust has continued to shape the acceptance of technologies for aging. Yet, we still know little about if or how to design for older adults' distrust in technology. Towards this, we reviewed empirical studies in which older adults discussed trust and distrust in relation to technology. Our analysis shows that (dis)trust is constituted in three key ways: it emerges when material arrangements of digital systems become illegible, such as when older adults cannot determine where information is stored or trace its movement; it is temporally constituted and fluid, building and shifting through prior experience and breakdowns; and at times distrust can operate as boundary work through which individuals limit technology's reach into their autonomy, relationships, and everyday lives. Our work yields design directions for supporting trust across different phases of technology use and by designing systems that make the movement and persistence of information more perceptible.
